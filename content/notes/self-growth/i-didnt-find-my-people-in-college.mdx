Recently, I visited a friend from high school at Princeton University. I tried
hard not to be jealous, I really did. But as I saw the beautiful Gothic
architecture, the lawn where tons of college students were lounging, and her
glamorous eating club, a pit formed in my stomach.

This was what _I had_ wanted from my own college experience. I imagined going to
a quaint school in the middle of nowhere, with beautiful buildings and a
stunning, Harry Potter-esque library. I imagined running into friends in the
dining hall, spending late nights studying in my collegiate Gothic dorm room,
and walking across a stage at graduation. I imagined having a school where
people actually wore the merch unironically.

I didn't get any of that.

At NYU graduation, you don't walk – you just stand while they put your picture
up on a big screen. I didn't go. We don't have a traditional campus; the
buildings are loosely scattered around Washington Square Park. On my walk to
class, I was more likely to run into any number of people – a man running a
halal cart, a nonprofit canvasser, or corporate workers rushing to the office –
rather than another student. The community I had craved for so long and thought
I would finally get, never came.

For a long time, I viewed this as a personal failure or a systemic rip-off. I
felt like I was missing out on the "real" version of being young and in college.

But, I've come to realize that, in many ways, the "traditional" college
experience is an extension of childhood – a curated, protective bubble designed
to delay the friction of real life. By not entering that bubble, I was forced to
step into a premature independence.

There is a certain kind of exhaustion that comes with having to figure out your
own life in Manhattan while your peers at "campus schools" have their meals
swiped and social calendars handed to them. I had to learn how to be okay with
not having the "best" version of the story. I had to learn that my worth wasn't
tied to the prestige of an eating club or the aesthetics of my library.

While I was mourning the lack of a campus, I was simultaneously learning how to
exist in the real world as an adult.

I learned how to:

    - **Navigate the Indifference**: The city doesn't stop for a big final presentation or an interview. I learned to advocate for myself in spaces that didn't care if I was a student or a CEO.
    - **Handle the Mid-Tier Moments**: I learned to focus in the library while knowing that the second I stepped outside, the "college" part of my day ended. I learned to stay grounded while walking past the raw chaos of the city—heated arguments, performers in the park, or the generic noise of the commute—and just keep going. It taught me that I didn’t need a manicured quad to decompress; I could find my own stability in the middle of a crowd that wasn't looking out for me.
    - **Sit with the Silence**: I had to be comfortable with the silence of not belonging to a "tribe". There was no school spirit to use as a personality trait.

So maybe I didn't "find my people" in the way that the brochures and Instagram
ads promised. I didn't find a group of thirty people who all wore the same
sweatshirt and sat at the same dining hall table for four years. But in the
middle of all that concrete and anonymity, I found something much more valuable:
**myself**. And as it turns out, the ability to stand on a New York street corner
and feel entirely at home in your own skin is a parting gift I wouldn't trade
for anything.
