  "Why we become who others expect us to be — and why escaping it costs more

For years, I found myself performing the quite, lonely, introverted girl,
without anyone even pressuring me to do so. Just because it felt like that was
who I was supposed to be. Because becoming someone with real, honest friendships
would require change, vulnerabiity, and awkward transitory periods that I didn't
know how to sit with.

And in other rooms, I found myself performing the complete opposite. I was fun,
cool, outgoing, and overly impulsive. This was the facade I had created when
we'd met, and I didn't want to potentially endanger the relationship by updating
that image.

These were two separate performances, and neither fully felt like me.

---

The strange part was that no one explicitly instructed me to act a certain way.
I'd walk into a room and immediately start scoping out the people. How did they
see me? What did they expect me to be like? What would disappoint them? I would
run through this thought experiment in my head, and then become whoever seemed
most suitable.

Sociologist Charles Horton Cooley called this the "looking-glass self" – the
idea that we shape our identity and self-concept based on how we imagine other
people perceive us.

But what happens when that imagination is driven by fear and a desperate need to
fit in? That's when the ought self is created. This concept, first coined by
psychologist E. Tory Higgins, is who you think you're supposed to be based on
what others expect.

Most people tend to embody the ought self (fear-driven), because evolutionarily,
fitting in with the group was critical to your survival.

Generally, this approach works. People accept you and the night continues
without incident. However, the problem with doing this continuously is that it
creates feelings of anxiety, guilt, and restlessness, Higgins found. You meet
expectations, but you can't shake the deep-seated feeling that this isn't _who
you actually are_.

It's like constantly having allergic reactions because everyone at the table
ordered something you're allergic to, and you don't want to be difficult by
speaking up about it. So you keep quiet, and bear the pain and discomfort of
your symptoms alone.

---

This disconnect between your actions and your belief about who you genuinely are
causes intense cognitive dissonance. Your brain is wired to make the discomfort
stop, using the least costly path possible. Changing your belief is easier than
changing your behavior, which requires consistent, long-term effort.

Eventually, you succumb to believing that the way you act might just be who you
actually are. And voila, the vicious cycle of the looking-glass self is
complete.

---

And while you were creating yourself, they were also choosing how to act based
on their perception of you. Prominent sociologist Erving Goffman called this the
"definition of the situation." He argued that within every social interaction,
participants construct a collectively maintained agreement about who everyone is
and how they are expected to behave.

If you were to show up differently, you wouldn't just be changing yourself.
You'd be disrupting an arrangement that everyone else has accepted as truth.

Doing this is threatening because people are wired to uphold this implicit
social contract. It gives them predictability about how the group will act, and
thus, how they should act and what they believe about themselves. When you
change, they feel unsettled. They pretend you didn't say that. They use tact to
smooth it over. They steer the conversation back to familiar ground. All to try
to restore the old version of you, because their comfort and, more importantly,
their self-concept in relation to you depends on it.

If you're the quiet follower and you start asserting your own opinions, the
leader of the group doesn't know how to lead anymore. The cost isn't just your
fear of rejection and your beliefs about yourself. If it were just that internal
work, changing would be difficult but still manageable. It's that even when you
are brave enough to change, the people around you are actively yet
unintentionally pulling you back. That's what makes this cost more than it
should.

By now, there are three forces working against you. You've built an identity
around what keeps you safe. You've internalized the performance as who you are.
And even if you were to try to change, the room itself would pull you back.
