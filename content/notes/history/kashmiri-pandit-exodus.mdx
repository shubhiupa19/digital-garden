## The Context

The entire Kashmir dispute starts with the independence of India in August
of 1947. The British colonizers, in a hastily-made exit plan, allowed all 565
princely states to either join India, join Pakistan, or remain independent based
on the borders they had drawn. It's important to note that this choice
technically belonged to the king of each state, not its constituents.

In the case of Jammu & Kashmir, Maharaja Hari Singh, who was a Hindu ruler but
ruled over a Muslim-majority population, decided to remain independent. However,
just a couple of months later, tribal fighters from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, armed
and supported by Pakistan, invaded Kashmir. They headed towards Srinagar, the
capital, while looting and committing violent crimes against people along the
way.

In a moment of panic and desperation, Singh asked India for help to ward off the
fighters. Then-Prime Minister Nehru agreed to provide military support, but on
one condition–Singh had to formally accede his territory to India. On October
26, 1947, the king signed the Instrument of Accession, and the next day Indian
troops flew in and fought off the raiders.

However, it's crucial to note that Nehru, who was shaped by anticolonial
idealism and self-determination philosophy, publicly committed to letting the
people of Kashmir ratify the accession once order was restored. He believed the
people would vote in favor of India due to his close relationship with Kashmir's
most popular leader, Sheikh Abdullah. He went to the UN Security Council in
1948, expecting them to condemn Pakistan's clear acts of aggression.

Instead, the Security Council treated both parties as equal, and passed
Resolution 47, calling for a plebiscite between India and Pakistan, which would
be held only _after_ Pakistan completely removed troops from the region and
India reduced them to a minimum, which never happened.

## The History of the Kashmiri Pandits

Kashmiri Pandits are Hindu Brahmins who are indigenous to the Kashmir valley.
Kashmir was a major center of Hindu and Buddhist scholarship for centuries
before Islam arrived in the 1300s.

When Islam was introduced to the valley, it came in waves, partly through Sufi
missionaries (peaceful) and partly through brutal rulers like Sultan Sikander.
Many Pandits either fled or converted during these times. However, a significant
community remained, blending with Muslim traditions to create a fusion Kashmiri
culture called Kashmiriyat. They spoke the same language, attended each other's
festivals, and dressed similarly.

But centuries of conversion and emigration were still highly evident. By the
late 20th century, the Pandits were about 140,000-170,000 of a valley of
millions. Despite their indigenous roots, they were a minority in their own
homeland.

## The Growing Tensions

The catalyst of the violence in the 1980s was the Kashmir state election
in 1987. The Muslim United Front (a coalition of political parties) ran against
the National Conference-Congress coalition. The MUF was genuinely popular and
posed a threat to the ruling party, so they rigged the election in order to stay
in power.

The National Conference-Congress was supported by Rajiv Gandhi's central
government in Delhi, and they wanted a leader they could control in Kashmir.
However, their attempt to protect India by rigging the elections not only showed
many residents that their voices didn't matter, but also provoked violent
insurgency.

Many future militant leaders, including Yasin Malik, were originally candidates
or campaign workers for the MUF. Seeing democracy fail them, they were incited
to use different means to raise awareness of their situation.

The initial insurgency, led by the JKLF (Jammu & Kashmir Liberation Force), was
largely secular; their goal was to separate from India, not join Pakistan.
However, specific Islamic sects within the organization, many who were directly
backed by Pakistan's intelligence agency (ISI), were steadily growing, and
_their_ vision was explicitly religious.

Then, in late 1989, the violence began. It started with targeted killings: Tika
Lal Taploo, a BJP leader who advocated for Kashmir's integration with India, was
shot in September 1989. Justice Neelkanth Ganjoo, who sentenced JKLF founder to
death, was shot in broad daylight at a market in Srinagar in November. These
killings, among others, sent a clear message: any Kashmiri who was associated
with India had a target on their back.

## January 19th and the 1990 Exodus

Eventually, rising tensions led to one fateful night: January 19th, 1990. First,
electricity was cut in Hindus neighborhoods, leaving them shrouded in darkness.
Then, thousands of mosques broadcasted terrifying messages all night long from
the loudspeakers of their mosques: most notably, "_Raliv, Tchaliv, Ya Galiv_" –
Convert, Leave, or Die. Other slogans included "_Kaafiro ko maaro_" (kill the
infidels), "_Yahan Nizam-e-Mustafa chalega_" (the rule of Allah will reign
here), and, worst of all, demands for a Kashmir without Pandit men – but with
their women.

Pandits huddled in their homes, terrified for their lives.

After midnight, Muslim mobs came out on the streets where Pandits lived and
threatened their lives with extreme slogans. These were not foreign militants,
but rather neighbors and friends of the Hindus. Overnight, their attitudes
seemed to have changed from peaceful and kind to suspicious and hostile.

By the next morning, January 20th, the police had vanished, the government had
collapsed, and the entire area was informally ruled by Islamists and mosques.
Chief Minister Farooq Abdullah had resigned that same day in protest of New
Delhi appointing a new governor, leaving no elected government at the exact
moment the Pandits needed one most. Posters appeared on Pandit homes. A local
Urdu newspaper, Al Safa, told Pandits to leave within hours if they wanted to
save their lives.

In the ensuing weeks and months, Kashmiri Hindus fled en masse, taking only what
they could carry. Most assumed the evacuation was temporary – that they'd be
back within weeks. In the neighborhood of Safakadal, the Pandits were showered
with coins and and funeral sweets – a ritual reserved for the dead in Islam.

They ended up in refugee camps in Jammu in atrocious conditions. Families were
crammed into single tents with little privacy, surviving off rationed water. By
mid-1990, nearly 100,000 Pandits had fled the valley.

As for the people who stayed, the Muslim militants kept their promise of death.
Hundreds of Pandits died or disappeared from that night onwards, and the
accounts of the murders were brutal – Sarwanand Koul Premi, a scholar who had
translated the Bhagavad Gita into Kashmiri, was dragged from his home along with
his son and shot in April 1990. The most chilling story was of Girija Tickoo, a
school librarian who was abducted, raped, and murdered while reportedly being
sawed in half with a carpenter saw, while she was still _alive_.

## The Broken Argument for Self-determination

A UN commission defined ethnic cleansing as "rendering an area ethnically
homogeneous by using force or intimidation to remove persons of given groups
from the area". By these standards, what happened to the Kashmiri Pandits was
clearly ethnic cleansing.

Before 1990, there were around 140,000 to 170,000 Pandits in the valley; by
2010, only ~3,400 people remained. Over 97% of Pandits were removed. An
indigenous community that had existed in the region for millennia was wiped out
in a matter of months. And the same people who did this were the ones demanding
Kashmir's right for self-determination. They disguised their fight behind the
banner of Kashmir's right to choose its own future, while driving out the
Kashmiris who would've chosen differently. Yes, Kashmiris have a right to
self-determination, but how can they fairly do so when the "self" no longer
exists?
