### The Original Act (1528)

1. Babur, the founder of the Mughal Empire in India, built a mosque on a site in
   Ayodhya, which Hindus believe to be Ram Janmabhoomi, or the birthplace of
   Lord Ram, who is one of the most central figures in Hinduism.
2. Ayodhya is one of the most holy cities for Hindus — it's what Mecca is to
   Muslims or Jerusalem is to Jews.
3. Building this mosque was part of a broader campaign by Mughal conquerors,
   primarily to assert political dominance over Hindus.
4. The archaeological evidence supports the Hindu claim; excavations of the site
   found evidence of a non-Islamic structure underneath the mosque.

### Centuries of Dispute

- Hindu worship on the perimeter of the mosque continued for centuries. In 1857,
  a chabutra (raised platform) was installed in the mosque's courtyard at the
  exact spot believed to be Ram's birthplace, showing that Hindu religious
  activity continued despite the mosque's existence.
- During the British Raj, the colonial government created a wall to divide the
  site: inner courtyard for Muslims, outer courtyard for Hindus. However, this
  was not a permanent resolution but rather a temporary management strategy. It
  didn't fully satisfy any party.

### 107 Years of Legal Efforts

- **1885:** Mahant Raghubir Das filed the first court case seeking permission to
  build a temple on the chabutra adjacent to the mosque. The court rejected it
  while still acknowledging the validity of the Hindu claim. The judge noted
  that it was "most unfortunate" that a mosque had been built on a land so
  sacred to Hindus, but said that too much time had passed to meaningfully
  rectify it. From the very first legal event, the courts essentially told Das
  he was right but they couldn't help him.
- **1949:** Idols of Ram appeared inside the mosque overnight on December 22.
  Whether divinely manifested or placed there by activists is debated. The
  government's response was to shut down the site and declare it a "disputed
  area." Muslim prayers ceased to occur here, meaning the mosque was
  non-functional as a place of worship for _43 years_ at the time of the
  demolition.
- **1950–1961:** Multiple civil suits continued to be filed. Paramahansa
  Ramachandra Das sued for the right to continue worship (1950). Nirmohi Akhara
  sued for possession (1959). The Sunni Central Waqf Board counter-sued for
  possession (1961). These cases entered the Indian judicial system and stayed
  unresolved for decades.
- **1986:** A district judge ordered the site to be unlocked for Hindu worship.
  For Muslims, this was seen as Rajiv Gandhi's government appeasing Hindu
  voters. For Hindus, this was a partial correction to a centuries-long
  injustice. Regardless of viewpoint, it escalated tensions dramatically between
  the two groups.
- **1989:** All lawsuits were raised to the Allahabad High Court. The Vishva
  Hindu Parishad (VHP) held a shilanyas (foundation-laying ceremony) with Rajiv
  Gandhi's compliance in order to earn Hindu votes — showing that even nominal
  support for Hindus was driven by political interests rather than genuine
  advocacy.
- **1990:** L.K. Advani started a rath yatra (political march) from Somnath to
  Ayodhya to incite Hindu sentiment nationally, transforming the issue from a
  local dispute into a national one. The yatra was stopped by Bihar CM Lalu
  Prasad Yadav and Advani was arrested before reaching his destination. Kar
  sevaks who did reach Ayodhya were fired upon — authorized by UP CM Mulayam
  Singh Yadav. Hindus were killed by Indian security forces for simply trying to
  reach their holy site.
- **1992:** By December 6th, Hindus had been seeking reparations for over 100
  years. Courts had verified their argument but refused to act. Politicians had
  used their issue to earn votes but changed nothing. Kar sevaks were murdered
  trying to reach the site. The mosque had been non-functional for 43 years. On
  this day, 150,000 kar sevaks gathered for a rally, which turned into the
  demolition.

### What the Demolition Actually Was

The Supreme Court acknowledged that what occurred was illegal mob violence.
However, the courts found that there wasn't adequate evidence that it was
"pre-planned," and concluded it was more likely a spontaneous event.

What is critical to remember is that this was a group of people reclaiming what
they believed to be their holiest site — supported by archaeological evidence —
after 464 years of dispossession and 107 years of failed legal proceedings. This
doesn't make it legal, but given the context, their actions start to make a bit
more sense.

### The Aftermath

The demolition incited riots all across India. Over 2,000 people died, mostly
Muslims. Dawood Ibrahim's 1993 Bombay bombings killed 257 more in retaliation.

Hindus paid a price too. They didn't lose as many lives, but were globally
branded as the aggressor community. The entire Hindu civilization's relationship
with its holy site was reduced to one image: a mob tearing down a mosque. People
conveniently set aside the centuries of temple destruction, the decades of
failed legal proceedings, and the archaeological evidence — all to hone in on a
single narrative.

### The 2019 Supreme Court Decision

In 2019, the Supreme Court gave the entire site to the legal representation of
the deity of Ram for constructing a temple. They also allocated a nearby 5-acre
plot for a new mosque. The court explicitly labelled the 1992 demolition as
illegal while simultaneously ruling that the Hindu claim to the site was
historically and archaeologically valid.

In January 2024, the Ram Mandir was finally consecrated. It took 496 years from
the mosque's construction, 139 years from the first court case, and 32 years
from the demolition to reach this point.

### The Bottom Line

No other community on Earth would have waited this long. The Crusades were
fought over exactly this kind of thing. The entire Zionist project exists
because Jews reclaimed ancestral sacred land. Saladin retook Jerusalem in under
90 years. But Hindus waited 464 years and filed court cases for 107 of them, and
got called extremists for it.

The demolition set off riots across India. Over 2,000 people died, mostly
Muslims. Then Dawood Ibrahim's network carried out the 1993 Bombay bombings,
killing 257 more in explicit retaliation. The violence was real and horrific.

But the way the world remembers this is completely lopsided. All of that history
— the centuries of dispossession, the courts acknowledging the claim but
refusing to act, kar sevaks getting shot in 1990 — gets compressed into one
image: a mob tearing down a mosque. The demolition became the beginning of the
story when it was actually the end of a much longer one.

And after 1992, if you were a Hindu who said "I get why it happened" — even
while acknowledging it was illegal — you were immediately labelled communal. The
space for a nuanced conversation just vanished. An entire community lost the
ability to talk about their own history without being called extremists. That
framing, which presents Hindus as senseless aggressors while erasing everything
that came before, is the core narrative injustice that persists today.
